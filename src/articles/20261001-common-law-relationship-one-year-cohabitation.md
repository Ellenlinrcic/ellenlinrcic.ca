# 同居满一年，就一定是 Common-law Partner 吗？

<p class="article-subtitle" style="font-size:22px; font-weight:700; line-height:1.45; color:var(--deep-green); margin:0 0 20px;">加拿大移民法中的“同居关系”，没有想象中那么简单</p>

<p><strong>If You Live Together for One Year, Are You Automatically Common-Law Partners?</strong></p>

<a class="language-jump" href="#english-version">Read in English</a>

在加拿大移民申请中，经常有人问：“我们已经住在一起一年了，是不是就自动变成 Common-law Partner？”也有人认为：“我们虽然住在一起，但没有共同账户、经济上各自独立，所以应该不算 Common-law。” 其实，这两种理解都可能过于简单。加拿大移民法中的 Common-law Partner，关键并不只是有没有住在同一个地址，也不只是简单计算“是否住满了12个月”。真正需要判断的是：两个人是否已经在一种 conjugal relationship（类似婚姻、具有长期承诺和相互依存性质的伴侣关系）中共同生活，并且这种共同生活持续至少一年。

## 一、法律真正要求的不是“同住一年”

《Immigration and Refugee Protection Regulations》（IRPR）第1(1)条对 Common-law Partner 的定义是：“an individual who is cohabiting with the person in a conjugal relationship, having so cohabited for a period of at least one year.”

也就是说，法律实际上包含两个核心要素：

Cohabitation（共同生活） + Conjugal Relationship（具有伴侣性质的相互依存关系）

并且这种状态持续至少一年。

因此：住在一起 ≠ 自动构成 Common-law。

两个室友可以共同居住很多年，当然不会因此成为 Common-law Partner。

即使两个人是男女朋友，共同居住本身也不是唯一的判断标准。真正需要进一步判断的是：他们在共同居住期间，是否已经建立了一种具有长期承诺和相互依存性质的伴侣关系。

IRCC目前的官方说明也使用了类似的表达：Common-law Partner 是已经连续至少12个月生活在 conjugal / marriage-like relationship 中的伴侣。

## 二、什么才算 “Conjugal Relationship”？

这是 Common-law 判断中真正复杂的部分。

IRCC在有关家庭团聚的指导中，将 conjugal relationship 描述为一种具有明显相互依存性质的关系，包括：

- physical（身体及生活上的亲密关系）

- emotional（情感上的依赖与支持）

- financial（经济上的关联与支持）

- social（社会生活上的结合）

同时还会考虑双方是否存在长期、真实、持续的 commitment，以及是否已经把彼此的生活在经济和社会层面结合起来。

这意味着，Common-law 并不存在一个简单的“打勾清单”。

例如，没有 Joint Bank Account，并不意味着一定不是 Common-law。

同样，有 Joint Lease、共同支付 Hydro 或 Internet，也不意味着一定就是 Common-law。

因为室友也可以共同签租约、平摊房租和水电费。

Officer真正需要判断的是：

这些事实综合起来，是否说明两个人已经从普通恋爱关系发展成为一个相互依存、具有长期承诺的生活共同体。

## 三、经济关联重要吗？

重要，但不能孤立来看。

很多申请人会把 Common-law 理解成：“只要一起住一年就算。”

另一些申请人则走向另一个极端：“我们没有共同账户，所以不算。”

其实都不准确。

IRCC在评估 conjugal relationship 时会考虑双方是否存在 financial interdependence，例如共同承担生活费用、共同资产、共同财务决定、互相提供经济支持、保险受益人安排等。

但这些因素并不是要求每一项都必须存在。

有些伴侣即使已经共同生活多年，仍然各自保留自己的工资账户；这并不妨碍他们形成 Common-law Relationship。

反过来，一对刚刚开始恋爱的情侣为了节省住房成本共同租房，各付一半房租、各自管理自己的生活，也不能仅仅因为共同地址持续了12个月，就机械地认为双方一定已经建立了 Common-law Relationship。

IRCC过去的官方指导甚至特别指出：正在 dating、正在考虑结婚，或者只是开始共同居住、尝试发展关系的人，并不因此当然已经处于 conjugal relationship。

## 四、真正容易出问题的是：12个月究竟从哪一天开始计算？

这是很多申请中最容易被忽略的问题。

很多人会直接采用一个公式：搬到一起的日期 + 12个月 = Common-law 日期

但从法律定义来看，更准确的理解应该是：

开始以 conjugal relationship 的性质共同生活的日期 + 至少12个月 = Common-law status

因此，以下几个日期不一定相同：

Relationship Started Date  
↓  
Cohabitation Started Date  
↓  
Conjugal Cohabitation Started Date  
↓  
Common-law Relationship Established

例如，一对情侣可能2019年开始交往，2021年因为住房方便开始共同居住，但直到2022年才逐渐形成稳定的共同家庭、经济和生活上的相互依赖。

这种情况下，不能只看到“2021年开始住在同一个地址”，就停止分析。

真正需要确定的是：从什么时候开始，他们是在 conjugal relationship 中共同生活？

这才是 Common-law 一年计算的关键。

## 五、一个真实案件给我的启发

我处理过的一个真实案例很好地说明了这一点。一对伴侣虽然已经共同居住超过一年，但IRCC最初审查他们的居住记录和相关资料后，仍认为现有证据不足以确定双方在相关时间是否已经构成 Common-law Relationship，因此要求进一步提交详细的 Relationship Timeline。双方如实说明了共同生活、家庭开支及关系发展的情况后，Officer才认定他们早期的共同居住已经具有 conjugal relationship 的性质，并据此确定了 Common-law 日期。这个案例提醒我们：共同地址可以证明双方住在一起，但不一定能够单独证明这段共同生活从什么时候开始具有 conjugal relationship 的性质，而这个时间点恰恰可能决定法律上的 Common-law 日期。

<h2>If You Live Together for One Year, Are You Automatically Common-Law Partners?</h2>
<p>Common-Law Relationships Under Canadian Immigration Law Are More Complex Than They May Seem</p>
<p>One of the most common questions in Canadian immigration practice is:</p>
<p>“We have lived together for one year. Does that automatically make us common-law partners?”</p>
<p>Others approach the question from the opposite direction:</p>
<p>“We live together, but we do not have a joint bank account and we keep our finances separate, so we are not common-law, right?”</p>
<p>Both assumptions can be overly simplistic.</p>
<p>Under Canadian immigration law, determining whether two people are common-law partners involves more than simply looking at whether they share an address or counting twelve months from the date they moved in together.</p>
<p>The real question is whether they have been cohabiting in a conjugal relationship for at least one year.</p>
<h3>Living Together Is Not, by Itself, Enough</h3>
<p>Section 1(1) of the Immigration and Refugee Protection Regulations (IRPR) defines a common-law partner as:</p>
<p>“an individual who is cohabiting with the person in a conjugal relationship, having so cohabited for a period of at least one year.”</p>
<p>The definition therefore contains two important elements:</p>
<p>Cohabitation + Conjugal Relationship</p>
<p>and that combination must continue for at least one year.</p>
<p>This distinction matters.</p>
<p>Two roommates can live at the same address for five years without becoming common-law partners. Even when two people are romantically involved, sharing a residence does not necessarily answer the legal question.</p>
<p>The nature of their relationship while they are living together must also be considered.</p>
<h3>What Is a “Conjugal Relationship”?</h3>
<p>This is where common-law determinations become more complicated.</p>
<p>There is no single document or factor that automatically establishes a conjugal relationship. The relationship must be considered as a whole.</p>
<p>In assessing whether a relationship is conjugal in nature, relevant factors may include the couple’s:</p>
<ul>
<li>physical and emotional relationship;</li>
<li>financial arrangements and degree of financial interdependence;</li>
<li>household arrangements and division of responsibilities;</li>
<li>social activities and how they present themselves to family and friends;</li>
<li>mutual support during illness or significant life events;</li>
<li>shared decision-making;</li>
<li>long-term plans and commitment to one another.</li>
</ul>
<p>The question is ultimately whether the couple has developed the type of mutual interdependence and commitment associated with a marriage-like relationship.</p>
<p>This is why there is no simple checklist.</p>
<p>A couple does not necessarily need a joint bank account to be common-law partners.</p>
<p>At the same time, having a joint lease or splitting utility bills does not automatically make two people common-law. Roommates can do exactly the same thing.</p>
<p>The evidence must be considered together.</p>
<h3>How Important Is Financial Interdependence?</h3>
<p>Financial interdependence can be an important indicator of a conjugal relationship, but it should not be considered in isolation.</p>
<p>Some couples maintain separate salary accounts even after years of living together. That does not necessarily prevent them from being common-law partners if the rest of their lives demonstrate substantial mutual commitment and interdependence.</p>
<p>Conversely, two people may split rent and household expenses simply because they live together. Those arrangements alone may say relatively little about whether their relationship has become conjugal.</p>
<p>Relevant financial evidence may include joint accounts, shared expenses, financial support, joint assets or liabilities, insurance beneficiaries and financial decisions made with the interests of both partners in mind.</p>
<p>But the real issue is not whether a particular financial document exists.</p>
<p>It is what the overall financial and household arrangements tell us about the nature of the relationship.</p>
<h3>When Does the 12-Month Period Actually Begin?</h3>
<p>This is one of the most overlooked issues in common-law cases.</p>
<p>It is tempting to use a simple formula:</p>
<p>Move-in Date + 12 Months = Common-Law Date</p>
<p>But that is not necessarily the correct analysis.</p>
<p>The regulatory definition requires the couple to have been cohabiting in a conjugal relationship for at least one year.</p>
<p>This means several dates may be different:</p>
<p>Relationship Start Date<br>↓<br>Cohabitation Start Date<br>↓<br>Conjugal Cohabitation Start Date<br>↓<br>Common-Law Relationship Established</p>
<p>For example, a couple might begin dating in 2020 and move into the same residence in 2022 for convenience or financial reasons. Their lives may initially remain largely separate.</p>
<p>Over time, however, their relationship may develop into one characterized by shared responsibilities, mutual financial and emotional support, long-term commitment and an increasingly integrated household.</p>
<p>In such circumstances, simply taking the original move-in date and adding twelve months may not fully answer the legal question.</p>
<p>The more important question is:</p>
<p>When did they begin cohabiting as conjugal partners?</p>
<h3>A Real Case Illustrates the Difference</h3>
<p>A case I handled illustrates why this distinction matters. The couple had lived together for more than a year, but when IRCC first reviewed their records, the officer found there was insufficient information to determine whether they had been common-law partners during the relevant period. IRCC therefore requested a detailed Relationship Timeline. After the couple provided further information about how they lived together, shared household expenses and developed their relationship, the officer concluded that their earlier cohabitation had already been conjugal in nature. The lesson is simple: a shared address may establish cohabitation, but it does not necessarily establish when that cohabitation became conjugal—and that distinction can determine the legal common-law date.</p>
